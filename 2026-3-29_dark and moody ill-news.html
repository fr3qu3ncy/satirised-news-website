In Kenya, a bizarre black market trade has erupted where sneaky ant smugglers are selling giant African harvester ants for up to $220 each, sparking chaos among farmers whose crops and homes are being invaded by the prized insects.

The Ant-ics: Kenya's Ant Trade Takes a Turn for the Wild

In a bizarre incident that has left Kenyans scratching their heads, a group of sneaky ant smugglers have been caught red-handed trying to sell giant African harvester ants on the black market. Yes, you read that right – ant smuggling is a thing now.

The story begins in Gilgil, a sleepy agricultural town in Kenya's Rift Valley where the rainforest meets the savannah. During this time of year, giant African harvester ant queens take to the skies, leaving their anthills to mate with unsuspecting males. It's like a Kenyan version of spring break, but instead of bronzed bodies and beach parties, it's all about ants.

Enter our heroes – or rather, our villains – who have been quietly collecting these coveted queen ants in small tubes and syringes supplied by foreign buyers. One such buyer was Zhengyang Wang, a Chinese national who was recently caught at Nairobi's Jomo Kenyatta International Airport with 2,000 queen ants worth over $7,700.

But here's the thing: ant collectors are like crypto enthusiasts – they're all about the thrill of the hunt and the prestige that comes with owning the rarest, most sought-after species. In this case, it's the giant African harvester ant queens, which can fetch up to £170 ($220) on the black market.

As Dino Martins, a biologist based in Kenya, explains, "It's like they're trying to corner the market on ants." And indeed they are – with colonies living for over 50 years and producing hundreds of thousands of workers, it's like they're trying to create their own ant-army.

Meanwhile, Kenyan farmers are having to deal with ants raiding their crops and invading their homes. It's like they've declared war on humanity. "We've had to call in the professionals," says one farmer, "the ant-exterminators. They come armed with bug spray and a healthy dose of annoyance."

But fear not, for the Kenyan authorities are on the case – or at least, they're trying to be. The KWS (Kenya Wildlife Service) has been working tirelessly to crack down on the ant-smuggling trade, but it's like trying to catch a gazelle in a field of tall grass. They just can't seem to get a grip on these sneaky ants.

As one official puts it, "We're doing our best to keep up with the demand for these pesky creatures." But we all know what they say about the best-laid plans – and how they often go awry when faced with an onslaught of ants.
