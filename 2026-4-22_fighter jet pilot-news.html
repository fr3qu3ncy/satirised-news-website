"YOU WON'T BELIEVE WHAT KILLED THESE 2 FIGHTER JETS (Hint: It Wasn't What You Think!)"

Two South Korean fighter jets collided in mid-air because their pilots were too busy taking photos and videos to fly straight, apparently prioritizing Instagram over air traffic safety.

FLYING FIASCO: PILOTS TAKE PICTURES, PLANES COLLIDE

In a bizarre incident that has left the world wondering what could possibly go wrong when fighter jets collide in mid-air, South Korean authorities have revealed that two F-15K jets crashed because their pilots were too busy taking pictures and videos to fly straight.

According to sources, the wingman pilot had declared his intent to take photos of the flight mission to commemorate his last day with his military unit. Because, you know, nothing says "team-building" like a mid-air collision.

As the two jets flew back to their base, the wingman pilot started snapping away on his personal mobile phone, completely distracting himself from the task at hand (i.e., not crashing into another plane). His colleague, who was flying the lead aircraft, then asked another pilot to film a video of the wingman's jet, because why not?

This led to the wingman pilot making an ill-fated decision to fly his jet up higher and flip it so that he could get a better shot for his Instagram. Unfortunately, this maneuver brought the two planes perilously close together, resulting in a collision that damaged both aircraft.

The lead aircraft's pilot tried to rapidly descend to avoid a crash, but it was too little, too late. The incident occurred because the air force didn't properly regulate pilots' personal use of cameras, said Seoul's Board of Audit and Inspection in a report published on Wednesday.

The wingman pilot, who has since left the military to work for a commercial airline (because who wouldn't want to fly a 737 after crashing two fighter jets?), was fined 88 million won ($596,000; £440,500) by the air force. The fine covered only a fraction of the repair costs, which came in at a whopping 880 million won.
